# Structure cheat-sheets

Most stuck drafts aren't short of words; they are short of a shape. Pick a structure before you outline, and switch if the material fights it.

## Essay structures

:::tabs
:::tab "Classic"
**Use for:** an argument you want the reader to come round to.

1. **Hook**: a scene, a question or a surprising fact. One paragraph.
2. **Stakes**: why this matters to the reader, not just to you.
3. **The case**: three moves, each with evidence and an example.
4. **The turn**: the strongest objection, taken seriously.
5. **Landing**: what changes if the reader believes you.

**Watch out for:** a hook that has nothing to do with the landing.
:::endtab
:::tab "Braided"
**Use for:** a personal story that needs an idea to hold it up, or an idea that needs a story to make it felt.

1. Pick two or three strands: a personal story, some history or science, a scene in the present.
2. Alternate between them in short sections, each ending on a small question.
3. Let the strands touch more often as the essay goes on.
4. Bring them together in the last section. The ending should only be possible because every strand was there.

**Watch out for:** one strand doing all the work while the others decorate.
:::endtab
:::tab "Hermit crab"
**Use for:** a subject too raw, or too ordinary, to tell straight.

Borrow the shape of another kind of document: a recipe, a repair manual, an instruction leaflet, a lost-property list. The form holds the story, and the gaps between its sections do the work.

**Watch out for:** the form turning into a gimmick. If any other form would do just as well, this one isn't working.
:::endtab
:::tab "Explainer"
**Use for:** a topic readers keep asking about.

1. **The question**, in the reader's own words.
2. **The short answer**, in two sentences.
3. **The ladder**: each section answers the next obvious question.
4. **What it means for you**: practical and specific.
5. **What we still don't know.**

**Watch out for:** answering questions nobody asked.
:::endtab
:::tab "Newsletter issue"
1. **Lede**: a moment from this fortnight. No throat-clearing.
2. **The idea**: what that moment made you think.
3. **The story**: one person or one object that shows it.
4. **One thing to try**: small, specific, doable this week.
5. **Sign-off**, and one link.

**Length:** 1,200 to 1,600 words. Shorter is fine; longer needs a reason.
:::endtab
:::tab "Book chapter"
1. **Scene**: hands on an object, in the present.
2. **Backstory**: how this person came to this work.
3. **The craft**: what the work is actually like, in detail.
4. **The widening**: what this craft says about the rest of us.
5. **Return**: back to the scene, changed by what we now know.
6. **The question** the reader carries out.

**Rhythm:** alternate scene and reflection; never two reflections in a row.
:::endtab
:::endtabs

## Openings

| Type | Example | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| In the scene | "Teodor holds the boot up to the window like a jeweller with a stone." | You have a person and a moment |
| The odd number | "Last year I threw away four kettles." | You have a fact that surprises |
| The admission | "I have never fixed anything that stayed fixed." | The piece is personal and the reader needs to trust you |
| The question | "Why does a new kettle cost less than fixing the old one?" | The piece is an explainer or an argument |

Avoid dictionary definitions, "in today's world", and the weather, unless the weather is the story.

## Endings

- **Return**: come back to the opening image, changed.
- **Open question**: hand the reader something to think about on the way to work.
- **The small act**: one thing to do. Works best in the newsletter.
- **The quiet line**: a short sentence after a long paragraph. Once a month at most.

## Headlines

Write ten, pick one. The first five are usually the ones anybody would write.

| Pattern | Example |
|---|---|
| The plain promise | How to darn a sock so it lasts another winter |
| The tension | Why a new kettle costs less than fixing the old one |
| The specific number | Eleven years, three resoles, one pair of boots |
| The overheard line | "You don't fix a shoe" |
| The reversal | The case against throwing it away |
| The reader's question | Is it worth repairing cheap things? |

**Rules:** headlines under 70 characters; the subtitle does the explaining, in under 120. Email subject lines stay under 50 characters and must still make sense on a phone's lock screen.
