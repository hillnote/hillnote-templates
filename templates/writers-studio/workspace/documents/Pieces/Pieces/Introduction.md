**One line:** why a book about repair, why now, and who it is for.

**The job of the introduction:** make the reader remember the first broken thing they threw away, then promise them twelve people who would have kept it going.

## Outline

1. **Open on the kettle.** Fourteen months old, dead on a Tuesday. The repair quote, the cheaper new one. Throwing it away anyway, and feeling strange about it.
2. **The question.** When did fixing things become the expensive option?
3. **What this book is not.** Not a guide. Not a lecture about waste. Twelve portraits of people who still fix things.
4. **The map.** Three parts: Hands, Places, People.
5. **The invitation.** Read it with something broken on the table.

## Draft excerpt

The kettle died on a Tuesday, fourteen months after I bought it. I know because I found the receipt, which is the kind of thing you do when you are trying to decide whether to be angry. The shop would repair it, they said, for slightly more than a new one. I bought the new one. Then I sat in the kitchen with the old kettle on the table in front of me, perfectly whole and perfectly useless, and felt that something had gone wrong that was bigger than a kettle.
