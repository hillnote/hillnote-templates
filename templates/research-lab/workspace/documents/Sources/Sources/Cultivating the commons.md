## Summary

A book comparing how six urban gardens are run. Gardens with open meetings, rotating roles and a share of plots kept for newcomers had more varied membership and fewer disputes. Gardens run by a small founding committee tended to stay closed to outsiders.

## Key claims

1. Shared, rotating governance widens who takes part. Logged as [[Shared governance widens who takes part|doc:documents/Findings/Claims%20and%20evidence/Shared%20governance%20widens%20who%20takes%20part.md]].
2. Visible rules help newcomers. Unwritten norms keep them out.

## Method

Six comparative case studies based on the author's fieldwork and documents. The author is an advocate for community land, which she states openly. There is no comparison of outcomes beyond membership.

## Quotes

> "A garden is governed whether or not anyone admits to governing it." (p. 88)

## My thoughts

Weak evidence by design, but the most practical source for RQ3: it turns "who is left out" into choices the committee can actually make. Chapter 7's checklist could go almost straight into the briefing as design principles. #access-and-equity
