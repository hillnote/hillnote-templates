# Question and scope

> Do community gardens improve the wellbeing of the neighbourhoods around them, and who benefits?

:::toc "On this page"
:::

## Why this study

The parks committee is deciding whether to fund three new community gardens. Supporters say gardens bring neighbours together and improve health. Sceptics say the benefits stay with a small group of keen gardeners. This study, **${study_title}**, brings together what the research already says and adds interviews from three existing gardens, so the decision rests on evidence rather than enthusiasm.

## Research questions

1. **RQ1.** What does existing research say about community gardens and the wellbeing of the people who use them: mental health, physical activity and diet?
2. **RQ2.** Do the benefits extend beyond gardeners to the surrounding neighbourhood, through social ties, a sense of safety or shared space?
3. **RQ3.** Who is left out, and which design and governance choices change that?

**Working hypothesis (H1):** community gardens improve wellbeing for the whole neighbourhood, not only for the people who hold plots. The Claims and evidence database marks every claim as supporting, qualifying or contradicting H1.

## Key definitions

- A [community garden]{#community-garden} is land gardened collectively or in individual plots by local residents, open to the neighbourhood at least some of the time. Allotments count; private and school gardens don't.
- [Wellbeing]{#wellbeing} covers mental wellbeing (life satisfaction, stress, mood), physical health behaviours (activity, diet) and social wellbeing (ties, trust, belonging).
- The [neighbourhood]{#neighbourhood} is everyone living within roughly 400 metres of a garden, about a five-minute walk.

## Framing the search

The question has a qualitative heart, so the search uses SPIDER rather than PICO:

| Element | This study |
|---|---|
| **S**ample | Urban residents, gardeners and non-gardeners |
| **P**henomenon of interest | Community and allotment gardens |
| **D**esign | Any: surveys, cohorts, trials, interviews, ethnography, reviews |
| **E**valuation | Wellbeing outcomes as defined above; experiences of access |
| **R**esearch type | Qualitative, quantitative and mixed methods |

## Inclusion and exclusion criteria

| Include | Exclude |
|---|---|
| Urban or suburban settings | Rural and farm settings |
| Peer-reviewed studies, theses, books and credible grey literature | Opinion pieces with no data |
| Published 2010 onwards (older if foundational) | Studies with no wellbeing or access outcome |
| English language | [Clinical or therapeutic settings]{#clinical-exclusion} (hospital wards, rehabilitation) |
| | School gardens and private gardens |

Screening runs in two passes: title and abstract first, then full text. Every full-text exclusion gets a row in the Sources database with status **Excluded** and a reason, so the decision can be checked later.

## What this study won't do

- Estimate the cost-effectiveness of gardens. That needs a different study.
- Rank individual gardens or name residents. Interviewees are pseudonymised as P01 to P12.
- Prove cause and effect. Most of the evidence is observational, and the review says so plainly.

## Deliverables

1. A literature review of about 6,000 words (the [[Literature review draft|doc:documents/Synthesis/Literature%20review%20draft.md]] is the working copy)
2. A two-page briefing for the parks committee
3. A themes-and-gaps summary that the committee can use to design the new gardens
