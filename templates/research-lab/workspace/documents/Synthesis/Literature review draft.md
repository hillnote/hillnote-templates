## 1. Introduction

Cities are often asked to fund community gardens on the strength of two promises. The first is that gardening makes people healthier and happier. The second, larger promise is that gardens bring whole neighbourhoods together. This review asks whether the evidence supports the second promise: do the benefits reach the whole neighbourhood, or stay with the people who hold plots? It is the literature strand of **${study_title}**, and it sets out what the research says and where it runs out.

## 2. How the literature was found

Three bibliographic databases, Google Scholar and the grey literature were searched using four concept blocks (garden, wellbeing, social, access), followed by backward and forward citation chasing. Of 498 records, 51 were read in full and ten have been included so far. The full search strategy and screening decisions are in the [[Search log|doc:documents/Question/Search%20log.md]]; inclusion criteria are in [[Question and scope|doc:documents/Question/Question%20and%20scope.md]].

The evidence is mostly observational. There is one prospective cohort study, one systematic review, one large survey of non-gardeners, and a set of case studies, theses and reports.

## 3. What the evidence says

### Restoration

[The clearest benefit is to gardeners' own mental wellbeing.]{#restoration} In a three-year cohort, new plot holders scored about 0.3 standard deviations higher on wellbeing than people still on the waiting list for the same sites, and the gap held at three years (Hartmann et al., 2019). Because both groups wanted a plot, this is better evidence than the usual comparison of gardeners with everyone else. It is, however, evidence about gardeners. ([[Claim|doc:documents/Findings/Claims%20and%20evidence/Taking%20up%20a%20plot%20improves%20mental%20wellbeing%20for%20gardeners.md]])

### Connection

[Gardens add social ties, but mostly among people who already know each other.]{#connection} Gardeners name more local contacts than non-gardeners, yet most new ties form between people who already knew each other by sight (Okafor & Lindgren, 2021). ([[Claim|doc:documents/Findings/Claims%20and%20evidence/Gardens%20mostly%20deepen%20ties%20among%20people%20who%20already%20knew%20each%20other.md]])

Benefits do reach some non-gardeners. Residents within 150 metres of a garden report more trust and belonging, but mainly when they talk to gardeners at least once a month (Castellanos & Whitfield, 2023). Interviews point to the same mechanism: the garden's boundary, its fence, gate and the produce left on the wall, is where the neighbourhood meets the garden. ([[Claim|doc:documents/Findings/Claims%20and%20evidence/Benefits%20reach%20non-gardeners%20through%20everyday%20contact%20at%20the%20fence.md]])

### Health and diet

[Diet and activity benefits are real but small.]{#health} Across 14 studies, gardeners ate about 0.6 more portions of fruit and vegetables a day, with smaller effects in the better-controlled studies (Mbeki et al., 2020). Gardeners also report more physical activity (Hartmann et al., 2019). Neither effect has been shown for neighbours. ([[Claim|doc:documents/Findings/Claims%20and%20evidence/Gardeners%20eat%20more%20fruit%20and%20vegetables%2C%20but%20the%20effect%20is%20small.md]])

### Access and exclusion

[Who benefits depends on who gets in.]{#access} Allocation rules, not lack of interest, explain who holds plots: a median wait of three and a half years, residency requirements and plots passed to relatives keep renters and newcomers out (Ramírez-Cole, 2022). Renters are also less likely to report any contact with a garden (Castellanos & Whitfield, 2023). Gardens with open meetings, rotating roles and plots reserved for newcomers have more varied membership (Adeyemi, 2018), though this rests on six case studies. ([[Claim|doc:documents/Findings/Claims%20and%20evidence/Waiting%20lists%20and%20tenure%20rules%20shut%20out%20newer%20and%20renting%20residents.md]])

### Change

[Over a longer timescale, gardens may change who lives in a neighbourhood.]{#change} Case studies of two districts suggest new gardens can feature in how an area is marketed as rents rise (Brooks & Haddad, 2024). No study yet measures rents near new gardens against comparable areas, so this remains a plausible risk rather than an established effect. ([[Claim|doc:documents/Findings/Claims%20and%20evidence/New%20gardens%20can%20speed%20up%20green%20gentrification.md]])

## 4. Discussion

The evidence partly supports H1. Gardens clearly benefit gardeners, and some benefits spill over to neighbours, but they spill over unevenly: to people who live closest, talk to gardeners and already belong.

| Theme | Direction for H1 | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Restoration | Supports, for gardeners | Moderate |
| Connection | Mixed | Moderate |
| Health and diet | Supports, weakly | Low |
| Access and exclusion | Contradicts | Moderate |
| Change | Contradicts, long term | Low |

Two design levers decide how far benefits spread: the **boundary** (how open and visible the garden is to passers-by) and **access** (who can get a plot, and how fast).

## 5. Gaps

No study follows non-gardeners over time, renters are almost absent from the evidence, and nobody has measured rents near new gardens. The full list, with next steps, is on [[Themes and gaps|doc:documents/Synthesis/Themes%20and%20gaps.md]].

## 6. What this means for the parks committee

1. **Design the boundary.** Low railings, a bench at the gate, a shelf for spare produce.
2. **Ration plots fairly.** Reserve a share of plots for new applicants and set a maximum tenure.
3. **Govern in the open.** Publish the rules, hold open meetings and rotate roles.
4. **Watch the rents.** Agree a baseline now so any change can be measured.
